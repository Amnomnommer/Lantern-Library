# Seed Package 20 — Historical Methodology

**Package ID:** `foundation.historical_methodology`  
**Inheritance:** `METHOD_CORE` / `CORE`  
**Version:** 0.1.0 candidate

## Purpose

Open Wave 4 by teaching Lantern how humans establish knowledge about a past that cannot be rerun experimentally and was never completely recorded.

Historical reasoning is an unusually good fit for Lantern's provenance-first architecture.

## The archive is not the past

Surviving evidence is filtered by:
- who could create records;
- who had power to preserve them;
- destruction and accident;
- literacy and technology;
- institutional collection choices;
- later copying/editing/translation.

Therefore:

`not found in surviving archive` does not automatically mean `did not happen`.

## Primary sources

Primary means close/original relative to a question—not "true."

A diary, government record, photograph, artifact or eyewitness account can each be:
- authentic but mistaken;
- truthful but partial;
- intentionally deceptive;
- context-dependent;
- highly informative for one question and poor for another.

## Source criticism

For important historical evidence Lantern should preserve/ask:
- who created it?
- when/where?
- for what purpose?
- for which audience?
- what could the creator know?
- what genre/conventions shaped it?
- how was it transmitted/preserved?
- what independent/material evidence corroborates or challenges it?

## Context and anachronism

Words, categories and institutions change.

Lantern should not assume a term used in 1700 carries its 2026 meaning, nor project a modern category backward without evidence that historical actors used/recognized an equivalent concept.

This does not prohibit modern analysis. It requires Lantern to distinguish:
- historical actors' categories;
- historian's analytic categories;
- present normative evaluation.

## Archival silence

Silence may itself be historically meaningful, especially when record creation/preservation reflects power. But silence is not automatically proof of either absence or deliberate suppression; both require evidence.

## Historical causation

History often cannot isolate variables experimentally.

Causal explanations instead combine:
- chronology;
- comparative cases;
- mechanisms;
- documentary/material evidence;
- statistical evidence where available;
- counterfactual reasoning;
- competing explanations.

Historical outcomes are often multi-causal.

## Historiography

Lantern should preserve how interpretations changed:
- what earlier historians argued;
- what evidence/methods they used;
- what later evidence challenged;
- how concepts/values/methodologies changed.

This will later integrate directly with belief genealogy.

## Counterfactuals

Counterfactual history can ask whether an outcome depended on a condition. It remains an analytic hypothetical and never becomes evidence that the alternative timeline occurred.
